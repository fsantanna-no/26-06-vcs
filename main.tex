}

\author {

\begin{abstract}
Public online forums suffer from abuse such as spam and misinformation.
Centralized platforms support moderation, but require full trust from users.
We propose Sybil-resistant peer-to-peer forums through a reputation system that
moderates content and delivers network consensus.
The design parallels Bitcoin:
    consolidated posts create reputation (as proof-of-work),
    likes transfer reputation (as transactions), and
    aggregate reputation determines consensus (as the longest chain).
Reputation depends on human \emph{proof-of-authoring}, which is slow and
scarce, thus suitable for consensus.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec.introduction}

Public online forums and social media are increasingly centralized in the hands
of a few companies, which moderate content and monetize user data over closed
protocols~\cite{internet.fixing,p2p.dosn}.
%
Open peer-to-peer alternatives eliminate intermediaries, but struggle to
achieve consistency when dealing with malicious users~\cite{p2p.osn,p2p.byz}.

By \emph{public forums} we mean any collaborative environment with multiple
readers and writers, such as chats, message boards, wikis, and code
repositories.
%
In an ideal forum, all posts satisfy three goals:
(i)   reach all users,
(ii)  are delivered in a consistent order, and
(iii) are respectful and on topic.
In centralized systems, (i) and (ii) are trivially achieved assuming service
availability, while (iii) requires users to trust their policies.
In peer-to-peer systems, however, none of these goals is easily accomplished.

Gossip protocols disseminate posts among peers, but do not guarantee consensus
due to conflicting delivery orders~\cite{p2p.intention,p2p.crdts}.
%
We argue that consensus is key to distinguishing malicious behavior \emph{at
the protocol level}~\cite{p2p.dag.sync}, and thus a requirement to satisfy
goal~(iii).
Without consensus, each client must rely on local anti-abuse policies (e.g.,
spam filters), which only apply \emph{a posteriori}, when the damage has
already been done.
%
Abuse is particularly acute in \emph{permissionless
networks}~\cite{permissionless,blockchain.wild}, in which membership is
decentralized, thus susceptible to Sybils~\cite{p2p.sybil,p2p.mastodon}.
%
To combat abuse and Sybils, and in support of goal~(iii), reputation systems
are a well-studied means to moderate content and build trust among
strangers~\cite{rep.systems,rep.eigentrust}.

Bitcoin~\cite{p2p.bitcoin} achieves permissionless
consensus~\cite{permissionless} by demanding a scarce resource
(proof-of-work~\cite{hashcash}) to write to its timeline, thereby resisting
Sybils.
%
However, we argue that cryptocurrencies in general are not suitable for social
content because they
    (a) enforce a unique global timeline,
    (b) lean towards concentration of power~\cite{btc.decentralization},
    (c) impose external economic costs, and
    (d) rely on objective rules to reach consensus.
%
A unique timeline that packs all forums under the same objective consensus
threatens decentralization and renounces subjectivity, which is inherent to
content moderation.
%
Cryptocurrencies also do not revoke payloads at the protocol
level~\cite{btc.content}, which is inadmissible considering illicit content in
public forums.

%~\cite{fcs.sbseg20,fcs.sbseg23}
In this work, we propose \FC, a permissionless peer-to-peer protocol with
integrated reputation-based consensus.
We adapt the idea of proof-of-work to resist Sybils and reach consensus, but
now in the context of public forums.

We highlight four main contributions (in italic):
%
We recognize human-authored content as a scarce resource, and reward members
with reputation tokens as a \emph{proof-of-authoring}.
%
Members can spend these tokens to rate and revoke posts as a
\emph{decentralized moderation} mechanism.
%
Tokens are intrinsic to each forum (unlike proof-of-work), which leads to
\emph{independent Sybil-resistant forums}, each with its own timeline and
etiquette.
%
Under network partitions, posts create forks in the forum graph, and a
\emph{reputation consensus}, resembling proof-of-stake~\cite{pos.ouroboros},
resolves conflicting operations (e.g., double-spending).
%This order also yields a deterministic chain time, rather than trusted clocks.
%
We implement \FC on top of unmodified Git, relying on hooks to add reputation,
moderation, and consensus.

As a main limitation, since partitions are unbounded, forums are subject to
long-range attacks, which we mitigate by settling local history after a
threshold (7 days)~\cite{pos.weak}.
%
As another limitation shared with blockchains, forum graphs are ever growing,
with costs to store and validate posts that increase over time.
Nevertheless, we simulated years of publicly available forum archives ranging
from chats to wiki articles, and (TODO: sync time, disk size).

In \FC, each topic or \emph{chain} is a replicated
\emph{Merkle DAG}~\cite{p2p.ipfs} whose cryptographic links encode causality
and ensure integrity among posts.
%
We build on top of Git as a familiar and solid peer-to-peer foundation, which
also provides content addressing, signing, deduplication, compression, and
replication.
%
Then, relying on non-intrusive hooks, we add what Git alone cannot provide:
reputation, moderation, and consensus.

The operation of a chain is simple: each action --- post, like or revoke --- is
an individual commit in the history DAG, which peers synchronize point-to-point,
with Git as the transport layer, as in gossip protocols~\cite{p2p.survey}.
%
Since state is a pure function of the immutable DAG, synchronized by appends
and unions, peers converge by construction (\S\ref{sec.design.sec}).
%
Table~\ref{tab.commands} lists the commands to operate a chain, which guide our
discussion through concrete examples.
%
Note that \FC is a protocol, so the \code{fc} commands are meant to be driven
by applications, not by end users.

The goals of this section are twofold:
    (a) to depict chain DAGs as the result of basic protocol operations; and
    (b) to illustrate how permissionless forums inevitably require Sybil
        resistance.
For the sake of simplicity, this section uses unrestricted chains that permit
unsigned posts.

\begin{lstlisting}[
    float=t,
    label={lst.basics},
    style=numbered,
    caption={Basic \emph{/chat} simulation in peer \emph{X}.}
]
# PEER X
$ fc /chat init

$ fc /chat post 'Hello World'
b100ce8ca42c1b1c366c41b764cad1bbc116cff8
$ fc /chat payload b100ce8

$ fc /chat metadata b100ce8

# Git backend
$ git -C /chat log --oneline

726e329 genesis 5596915440
\end{lstlisting}

Listing~\ref{lst.basics} simulates a peer \emph{X} (ln~1) that initializes
chain \emph{/chat} (ln~2--3), posts message \emph{``Hello World''} (ln~4--5),
and queries its payload (ln~6--7) and metadata (ln~8--11).
%
The action metadata includes its type, a self-declared timestamp, and a
reference to the payload blob.
Since revocation is a key moderation requirement (\S\ref{sec.design.votes}),
only the metadata lives in the immutable chain DAG, not its payload.

\FC follows the \emph{local-first} principle~\cite{p2p.local}, and operates
normally while offline, only affecting the local replica: no communication
occurs up to this point.
Since we build upon Git, we can inspect the internal structure of a given
chain (ln~13--16): the \emph{/chat} repository contains two commits, the
genesis with a random id, and the first post pointing to its payload.

\begin{lstlisting}[
    float=t,
    label={lst.clone},
    style=numbered,
    caption={Peer replication between \emph{X} and \emph{Y}.}
]
# PEER X
$ fc daemon start

# PEER Y
$ fc /chat clone X
726e32918b7ac760de4088a5959168bde5e5a608
$ fc /chat list dag

\end{lstlisting}

\begin{lstlisting}[
    float=t,
    label={lst.fork},
    style=numbered,
    caption={Forked DAG after concurrent posts.}
]
# PEER X
$ fc /chat post 'From X'
eb18b4527ee5ecc6106a086c35fbfe8c25fa6aba

# PEER Y
$ fc /chat post 'From Y'
c7a56bc491d415a8240b7012c0aae846fc632234

# PEER X or Y
$ fc /chat sync Y (or X)

      b100ce8           #    "Hello World"
      /     \           #      /       \
c7a56bc     eb18b45     # "From Y"   "From X"
\end{lstlisting}

To illustrate point-to-point synchronization, in Listing~\ref{lst.clone}, peer
\emph{X} starts listening for requests (ln~1--2, omitted in the next listings),
and a second peer \emph{Y} (ln~4) clones it by its address (ln~5--6), receiving
the single \emph{``Hello World''} post (ln~7--8).
%
In local-first protocols, replicas evolve separately and synchronize at some
point in the future.
In Listing~\ref{lst.fork}, each peer posts locally (ln~1--3 and 5--7) and
eventually synchronizes with the other side (ln~9--10), both converging to the
same forked DAG (ln~11--14), now closed with a merge commit (hidden since not
an action).

After the initial \code{clone}, a \code{sync} is the only moment two peers
connect: a short exchange of missing actions (a Git fetch and push), after
which the connection is closed.
The remote address needs to be known in advance, as with bootstrap contacts in
peer-to-peer systems~\cite{p2p.survey,p2p.scuttlebutt}.
There are no servers, brokers, discovery, broadcasts, or any underlying
network architecture in \FC: only explicit point-to-point ephemeral
connections.
In peer-to-peer terms, ``joining'' is cloning, ``leaving'' is simply not
syncing, and ``reconnecting'' is syncing again.
Whom to sync with, and how often, is left to users and applications, so
replicas may legitimately diverge for a long time
(\S\ref{sec.design.partitions}).

Note that forks represent concurrency and imply ambiguity in the order of
actions, which is a fundamental obstacle to consensus.
Naturally, in permissionless forums, we cannot trust members' timestamps to
order actions (\S\ref{sec.design.time}).
%
As another limitation, Merkle DAGs alone cannot provide Sybil resistance, since
any user at any peer could flood chain DAGs with abusive
posts~\cite{p2p.merkle-crdts,p2p.byz}.
%
The next section addresses both problems:
    reputation rules make posting scarce, and
    a consensus order resolves fork conflicts.

\section{Reputation and Consensus}
\label{sec.design}

In \FC, members spend tokens named \reps to post and rate content in forums:
    a \code{post} initially penalizes members, until it consolidates and counts
    positively;
    a \code{like} or \code{dislike} rates members and posts;
    a \code{revoke} or \code{unrevoke} moderates posts.
%
Table~\ref{tab.rules} lists all concrete rules, which we explain through
illustrative examples.
%
In the rest of this section, we detail the reputation system and how it works
in conjunction with the consensus mechanism to resist Sybils in permissionless
forums:

\item [\S\ref{sec.design.boot}]
    Chain Bootstrapping:
    how to admit the first members and dilute their initial power over time.
\item [\S\ref{sec.design.members}]
    Member Admission:
    how insiders welcome outsiders without intermediate authorities.
\item [\S\ref{sec.design.poa}]
    Proof-of-Authoring:
    how new content mints \reps and sustains a growing forum.
\item [\S\ref{sec.design.votes}]
    Content Rating and Revocation:
    how votes measure quality and combat abuse.
\item [\S\ref{sec.design.double}]
    Concurrency and Double-spends:
    how forks lead to inconsistent operations.
\item [\S\ref{sec.design.consensus}]
    Consensus and Total Order:
    how peers converge on \reps accounting.
\item [\S\ref{sec.design.time}]
    Chain Time:
    how to count time consistently without trusted clocks.
\item [\S\ref{sec.design.partitions}]
    Network Threats:
    how to defend from malicious peers and deliberate branches.
\item [\S\ref{sec.design.sec}]
    Convergence and Divergence:
    how replicas converge, and when they split.
\end{description}

Since we build upon unmodified Git, we argue that our design can be adapted to
any system that structures posts as a Merkle DAG.
For instance, the consensus is just a view of the underlying DAG, computed
locally by each peer to order and validate posts.

\subsection{Chain Bootstrapping}
\label{sec.design.boot}

To prevent Sybils, members with no \reps cannot operate on the chains, and
require a welcoming like from any other reputed member.
%
Here, we face a \emph{bootstrapping} problem, typical of permissionless
systems~\cite{rep.pseudonyms,p2p.sybil,p2p.bitcoin}:
    how to admit the first members,
    and how to decentralize power over time?
%
Our proposal is to designate \emph{pioneers} with initial \reps when creating
chains.
The pioneers shape the evolution of chains with the first posts and likes, and
gradually transfer \reps to new members, which dilutes their power, making
decentralization possible.
%
Therefore, our notion of permissionless forums is not that anyone can post at
will, but that any insider can welcome any outsider by directly transferring
\reps, without intermediate authorities.

\begin{lstlisting}[
    float=t,
    label={lst.pioneers},
    style=numbered,
    caption={Sybil-resistant chain with pioneers.}
]
# PEER I
$ ssh-keygen -f alice
$ ssh-keygen -f bob
$ fc /chat init --pioneer=alice --pioneer=bob
09ab434cde2924de9ac83fb5be261700629f5e07

# PEER J
$ fc /chat clone I
09ab434cde2924de9ac83fb5be261700629f5e07
$ fc /chat reps members
alice      25000
bob        25000

$ ssh-keygen -f silas
$ fc /chat post 'Hello forum' --sign=silas
ERROR : chain post : insufficient reputation
\end{lstlisting}

Listing~\ref{lst.pioneers} illustrates the process of bootstrapping and
replicating a fresh Sybil-resistant chain.
%
In peer \emph{I} (ln~1), we generate self-certifying keypairs for \emph{Alice}
and \emph{Bob} (ln~2--3) and set them as pioneers of a new chain \emph{/chat}
(ln~4--5).
%
Then, in peer \emph{J} (ln~7), we clone \emph{/chat} from \emph{I} (ln~8--9),
and inspect its pioneers (ln~10--12).
The total of \nreps{50K} (50,000 reputation tokens) is equally split, and each
pioneer starts with \nreps{25K} (\R{1.a}).
We also demonstrate that \emph{Silas} (ln~14), a potential Sybil, cannot write
to the chain (ln~15--16, \R{4.a}).

\subsection{Member Admission}
\label{sec.design.members}

\begin{lstlisting}[
    float=t,
    label={lst.welcome},
    style=numbered,
    caption={Member admission through likes and begging posts.}
]
$ fc /chat like 5000 member carol --sign=alice
$ fc /chat reps members
bob        25000
alice      20000
carol       4500

$ fc /chat post 'Great Post' --beg --sign=dave
5d98790763bb3ca93bb84985bd49c9fa0887fe6e
$ fc /chat list begs
5d98790763bb3ca93bb84985bd49c9fa0887fe6e
$ fc /chat like 4000 post 5d98790 --sign=bob
$ fc /chat reps members
bob        21000
alice      20000
carol       4500
dave        1300
\end{lstlisting}

Once initialized, the only way to welcome new members is through likes, which
transfer \reps between identities.
%
Likes reach newcomers in two complementary ways:
    (i) \emph{friend-to-friend}, where members target known newcomers directly;
        and
    (ii) \emph{begging posts}, where members target posts pending admission.
%
The second is more interesting, since it accepts total strangers based purely
on content quality.
%
Listing~\ref{lst.welcome} illustrates both:
%
(i) \emph{Alice} transfers \nreps{5000} to \emph{Carol} with a like (ln~1,
\R{3.c}), which, after a 10\% tax (\R{3.a}), yields \nreps{4500} (ln~2--5).
%
(ii) \emph{Dave} begs a post (ln~7--8), which lives off-chain (ln~9--10) until
\emph{Bob} likes it with \nreps{4000} (ln~11), which is split with the author
(\R{3.b}) after the tax, minus the temporary charge (\R{2}), yielding
\nreps{1300} to \emph{Dave} (ln~12--16).
%
Since likes are at best zero-sum (transfers and taxes do not add value),
cycling \reps among colluding Sybils gains nothing.

A begging post is a normal post commit that sits as a tip in a side branch,
waiting for a like that eventually merges it into the chain DAG.
Peers are not required to hold or forward beg branches, so a reasonable policy
is to keep and synchronize them long enough for strangers to be seen.

\subsection{Proof-of-Authoring}
\label{sec.design.poa}

Pioneers address bootstrapping, but the fixed initial supply of \reps (\R{1.a})
cannot sustain a growing chain with incoming members and posts.
%
Facing the same issue, Bitcoin mints new coins as a reward for computation
(\emph{proof-of-work}), but as an \emph{extrinsic resource} that concentrates
power.
For public forums, we propose human-authored content
(\emph{proof-of-authoring}) as an \emph{intrinsic resource}, which is still
analogous to mining:
    (i)   authoring is harder than rating;
    (ii)  new content adds value to chains; and
    (iii) the reputation it mints decides forks.

\begin{lstlisting}[
    float=t,
    label={lst.poa},
    caption={Proof-of-authoring: member reputation over time.}
]
$ fc /chat reps member carol     # T0: before post
4500
$ fc /chat post 'Nice Post' --sign=carol
1a5c8e21d934e6a44e47f72f188250faeaf0a6de
$ fc /chat reps member carol     # T1: after post
4000
$ fc /chat reps member carol     # T2: after tick
5500
\end{lstlisting}

Since new content is the \emph{raison d'être} of public forums, \FC rewards
members whose posts persist on the chain (\R{1.b}).
%
To illustrate proof-of-authoring, Listing~\ref{lst.poa} traces how
\emph{Carol}'s reputation evolves with a post:
%
\begin{description}
\item[T0:] \emph{Carol} holds the \nreps{4500} received from \emph{Alice}'s
    welcoming like (\R{3.c}).
\item[T1:] The new post costs \nreps{500} (\R{2}), which limits how often
    she can post.
\item[T2:] The cost is refunded at the next tick (\R{2}), which also
    consolidates the post and rewards her with \nreps{1000} (\R{1.b}).
    Each tick takes up to 24 hours, less under chain activity
    (\S\ref{sec.design.time}).
\end{description}

The 24-hour period gives time for other members to judge the post before the
reward.
More importantly, it regulates the emission of \reps, since extra posts do not
reward members further (\R{1.b}).

As a limitation, since \reps emission and cap apply per identity
(rules~\code{1.b}~and~\code{4.b}), a reputed attacker may seed several fresh
identities to multiply emission.
%
We argue that these are not Sybils in the strict sense, since each identity
must behave as an independent member to pass proof-of-authoring.

The actual threat is therefore junk content, human or AI-generated, which the
community must judge by its frequency and relevance, and revoke accordingly.

\subsection{Content Rating and Revocation}
\label{sec.design.votes}

Content rating is a defining feature of public forums, from Slashdot's scarce
moderation points~\cite{forum.slashdot} to Reddit's votes~\cite{forum.reddit}
and Stack Overflow's reputation~\cite{forum.stackoverflow}.

\begin{lstlisting}[
    float=t,
    label={lst.rating},
    style=numbered,
    caption={Content rating through likes and dislikes.}
]
$ fc /chat reps post 5d98790
1800
$ fc /chat like 1000 post 5d98790 --sign=alice
$ fc /chat dislike 2000 post 5d98790 --sign=carol
$ fc /chat reps post 5d98790
1350    # 1800 + 450 - 900
\end{lstlisting}

In \FC, each forum post holds its own rating as the balance between likes and
dislikes targeting it (\R{3.c}), which end-user software can use to filter and
highlight content.
%
Listing~\ref{lst.rating} shows the current \reps of \emph{Dave}'s post
(ln~1--2).
Then, \emph{Alice} likes it (ln~3) and \emph{Carol} dislikes it (ln~4).
After applying the taxes and split (rules~\code{3.a}~and~\code{3.b}), the final
outcome is \nreps{1350} (ln~5--6).

The economy of \reps induces members to only rate content they care about.
%
On the one hand, since \reps are finite, members think twice before spending
them (\R{3.c}).
On the other hand, since \reps are capped (\R{4.b}), members have an incentive
to rate content, which also dilutes their power.
%
These lower and upper limits work together towards the quality of forums.

\begin{lstlisting}[
    float=t,
    label={lst.revoke},
    style=numbered,
    caption={Content revocation of an abusive post.}
]
$ fc /chat post 'nice product' --sign=dave
65980b7b536eb850d05c06e1ca517cb7ca074257
$ fc /chat revoke 1000 65980b7 --sign=alice
$ fc /chat payload 65980b7
ERROR : chain payload : revoked
$ fc /chat unrevoke 1000 65980b7 --sign=bob \
    --file=product.txt
$ fc /chat payload 65980b7
nice product
\end{lstlisting}

Ratings alone cannot stop abuse, such as spam, misinformation, or even
illegal content.
For such cases, members may spend \reps to \emph{revoke} an action to erase its
payload in all honest peers (\R{3.d}).
%
In Listing~\ref{lst.revoke}, \emph{Dave} posts spam (ln~1--2), which
\emph{Alice} revokes (ln~3), making its payload unavailable (ln~4--5).
%
Since payloads live outside the immutable DAG, which references them by hash
only (\S\ref{sec.git}), peers can prove a revocation and refuse to forward
them.
%
Revocation is reversible through \emph{unrevoke} (ln~6--9), according to the
balance between votes.
Note that a copy of the payload must be provided, which is validated against
its hash persisted in the metadata.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{state}
\caption{
    Lifecycle of an action.
}
\label{fig.state}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig.state} summarizes the lifecycle of an action.
A new action from a member with enough \reps is immediately \emph{accepted}
into the chain (\R{4.a}).
Otherwise, it is \emph{begging} outside the chain until a like admits it
(\S\ref{sec.design.members}).
Once accepted, an action is part of the immutable history, but its payload
can still be \emph{revoked} and restored according to the balance of votes.

Revocation is deliberately expensive: it costs the revoker at least
\nreps{1000}, twice the minimum of other actions (\R{4.a}), and it drains the
target and its author (\R{3.b}).
It also cancels the reward of the revoked post (\R{1.b}), which is the
ultimate defense against farmed reputation (\S\ref{sec.design.poa}).
%
Hence, revokes are meant to combat abuse, whereas dislikes exist for
disagreement.
However, in either case they burn \reps on both sides, so antagonistic members
draining each other relentlessly may collapse the chain.

Finally, as a \emph{right to be forgotten}, authors may revoke their own posts
for free, which no other member can undo.

\subsection{Concurrency and Double-spends}
\label{sec.design.double}

As discussed in Section~\ref{sec.git}, concurrent actions from multiple
peers create forks in the DAG, which implies ambiguity in their order.
Therefore, scarcity of \reps alone is not sufficient to prevent inconsistent
actions, akin to \emph{double-spending} in Bitcoin.
For instance, consider a malicious member with just enough \reps for a single
action, trying to spend them twice from different peers.

\begin{lstlisting}[
    float=t,
    label={lst.double},
    style=numbered,
    caption={Double-spend attempt and rejection.}
]
$ fc /chat reps member carol
4500

# PEER I
$ fc /chat like 4500 member xion --sign=carol
7468f50551b223133f48365984d1a192e7a36a67

# PEER J
$ fc /chat like 4500 member york --sign=carol
3d5fbf9a7e22af44f7b26ac7392ff0e9a63711cd

# PEER I
$ fc /chat sync J
ERROR : invalid like : insufficient reputation
voided : 7468f50551b223133f48365984d1a192e7a36a67

$ fc /chat reps members
...
york        4050       # accepted
xion           0       # rejected
carol          0
\end{lstlisting}

In Listing~\ref{lst.double}, \emph{Carol} with only \nreps{4500} (ln~1--2)
tries to double-spend it with concurrent likes to \emph{Xion} and \emph{York}
on peers \emph{I} and \emph{J} (ln~4--6 and 8--10), just before they
synchronize (\S\ref{sec.git}).
%
Note that each like is legitimate in isolation, but inconsistent in the merged
DAG.
Therefore, when peer \emph{I} synchronizes with \emph{J} (ln~12--15), it
replays the DAG in topological order, accounts for the actions in sequence,
detects the conflict, and rejects one of them (ln~17--21).

As a consistency requirement, all peers must replay the DAG in the exact same
order to compute \reps in the same way.
In fact, due to the consensus mechanism we are about to discuss, the peers not
only refuse the conflicting like but also converge to the same state (here
keeping \emph{York}'s like by a tie break, \S\ref{sec.design.consensus}).

\subsection{Consensus and Total Order}
\label{sec.design.consensus}

In \FC, consensus determines a total order of all actions in the forum,
computed as a deterministic function of the chain DAG itself.
Peers holding the same DAG compute it locally in the same way, without
further votes or network coordination.
%
It resembles \emph{proof-of-stake}~\cite{pos.ouroboros,pos.weak}, but instead
of favoring wealth, we reward authors for adding content, which is valuable in
itself.

The key idea is to favor branches whose members hold the majority of the
reputation in the common history, which contains the actions that both branches
agree on.
%
When faced with a fork, we first determine the exclusive members of each
branch (common members are ignored).
To pick the winner, we compare the aggregate \reps each set held at the fork
point.
%
Finally, the winning branch is placed first as a whole and the losing one is
appended after it (regardless of self-declared timestamps,
\S\ref{sec.design.time}).
For nested or multi-way forks, we apply the same rule recursively or in
sequence, respectively.
%
In our implementation, peers cache consensus state after every commit, so that
replays restart from the snapshot at the common ancestor rather than from
genesis.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{dag}
\caption{
    (A) Forum DAG with common history and two branches.
    (B) Total order after consensus.
}
\label{fig.reps}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig.reps}.A illustrates a forum DAG forked by a temporary network
partition.
The common history has signed actions from members $a$, $b$, and $c$.
Up to the fork point, we assume that members $a$ and $b$ have more
contributions and therefore hold more \reps combined than $c$ has alone
($5+3>6$).
%
Then, member $c$ disconnects and keeps posting with new members $x$ and $y$ in
\code{branch-1}.
Meanwhile, $a$ and $b$ also participate with new posts in \code{branch-2}.
%
When the network reconnects and the DAG finally merges, Figure~\ref{fig.reps}.B
indicates the resulting consensus order, which considers $a$ and $b$ as
winners:
    first the common history,
    then all actions in \code{branch-2},
    then all actions in \code{branch-1}.

After determining the consensus order, we replay all actions in sequence to
check for inconsistencies.
%
Once an action fails (e.g., a double-spend), all subsequent actions in the
losing branch are rejected and erased (as if they never existed), since they
descend from it in the Merkle~DAG.
%
In Figure~\ref{fig.reps}.B, suppose that the last action by $a$ in
\code{branch-2} (in gray) is a dislike that leaves member $c$ with
insufficient \reps.
Then, its last post in \code{branch-1} (in red) is invalidated, together with
all subsequent actions.

We now recap the consensus mechanism and how it applies to
Figure~\ref{fig.reps}:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item Split members into disjoint sets at the fork point:   \\
    $M_1=\{c,x,y\}$ and $M_2=\{a,b\}$
\item Sum the \reps of each set in the common history:      \\
    $R_1=6$ and $R_2=8$
\item Order branches by highest sum first (tip hash as tie breaker):
    \code{common <- branch-2 <- branch-1}
\item Discard actions from the first failure on:            \\
    \code{c <- y} (last two actions)
\end{itemize}

Peers that received the losing branch first need to reorder actions after the
fork point, similarly to a blockchain reorganization.
Moreover, legitimate actions that are erased must be reposted by their
authors, which should be rare, since members are expected to gossip among
themselves often.
Note that erased posts never charge \reps, since they are no longer in the DAG,
as if they had never occurred.

\subsection{Chain Time}
\label{sec.design.time}

\FC faces the challenge of counting time across peers consistently without a
global clock:
    the 24-hour ticks (\R{1.b}, \R{2}) and
    the 7-day settling window (\S\ref{sec.design.partitions})
    depend on real time passing.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{times}
\caption{
    Timing tolerance window for new actions.
}
\label{fig.times}
\end{figure}

We adopt the principle of a \emph{chain time} based on immutable timestamps in
the chain, such that peers holding the same DAG compute the same durations.
However, in our permissionless context, we cannot trust self-declared
timestamps, since malicious members could manipulate them to collect rewards or
to vandalize chains.
%
Therefore, just before an action enters the DAG, the peer validates its
timestamp within reasonable bounds:
    no older than its newest ancestor, and
    no newer than the local clock (each with 1 hour of tolerance).

Figure~\ref{fig.times} illustrates the tolerance window to append new actions
into the DAG.
The solid shapes represent previously validated actions (\code{tip}, \code{b1},
\code{b2}).
The dashed shapes represent new actions and their outcomes considering the
tolerance window: the labels \code{ok} are accepted, whereas \code{old} and
\code{new} are rejected.
%
The lower limit (\code{ancestors-1h}) depends only on timestamps from
actions already in the chain, so its validation is deterministic across peers.
%
The upper limit (\code{local+1h}), however, is the only source of
non-determinism, since each peer runs its own clock.
Nevertheless, a peer whose clock is behind simply refuses syncs until a later
retry, when it converges to the same state.

Therefore, as in Bitcoin~\cite{btc.timestamps}, initial action admission is a
local decision, but once in the chain, decisions are deterministic based on
immutable parents, hence the term \emph{chain time}.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{monotonic}
\caption{
    Consensus timeline as monotonic clock and chain ticks.
}
\label{fig.monotonic}
\end{figure}

We also rely on chain time to derive a monotonic consensus timeline in the
spirit of Lamport's logical clocks~\cite{time.lamport}, but anchored in
timestamps as in Bitcoin~\cite{btc.timestamps}.
%
Each action in the total order carries a \emph{position timestamp}~($P_i$) as
the maximum between the previous position timestamp~($P_{i-1}$) and its own
self-declared timestamp~($S_i$): $P_i=\max(P_{i-1},S_i)$, with $P_0=S_0$.
%
Figure~\ref{fig.monotonic}.A illustrates a forked DAG with non-monotonic
timestamps, whereas Figure~\ref{fig.monotonic}.B shows its
consensus order as a monotonic clock.
For instance, note how timestamp $S_4=4$ in the losing branch (in gray) becomes
$P_4=12$ (much newer) when preceded by the winning branch.
We say that such a post is \emph{clamped}, since its position timestamp exceeds
its declared timestamp ($P_4 > S_4$).

The monotonic timeline determines how recent a losing branch is in the local
order rather than how old it claims to be.
This prevents losing branches from settling instantly when recovering from
network partitions (\S\ref{sec.design.partitions}).

Finally, the consensus timeline drives the ticks that refund and reward posts
(\R{1.b}, \R{2}), varying between 0 and 24h as follows:
\[
    24h \cdot \max(0,\, 1 - 2M/T)
\]
It considers the sum of \reps of the members acting since the tick started
($M$) over the total of \reps in the chain ($T$).
%
Figure~\ref{fig.monotonic}.C illustrates chain ticks:
    $T_1=12h$ as members $a$ and $b$ hold $25\%$ of the chain economy;
    $T_2=24h$ as the chain is idle;
    $T_3=0h$ as member $c$ holds the majority of \reps.
%
At each boundary, all posts from the previous tick are refunded (\R{2}), but
each author is rewarded at most once (\R{1.b}), regardless of extra posts
(gray $b$ at $T_1$).
%
Note that posts in losing branches are squeezed into a later tick (all
$P_i \geq 12$ in Figure~\ref{fig.monotonic}.B), which prevents farming \reps in
isolation (\S\ref{sec.design.partitions.farming}).

\subsection{Network Threats}
\label{sec.design.partitions}

In local-first peer-to-peer networks, partitions are not exceptional events,
but the norm:
    peers join, leave, and reconnect at will (\S\ref{sec.git}).
%
This contrasts with standard blockchains, which consider partitions as faults,
and assume an online majority that discards minority branches.
%
Partitions are also \emph{unbounded} and possibly \emph{malicious}, since no
one can distinguish whether the causes are deliberate or accidental.

Therefore, \FC does not treat partitions as attacks, but only reacts to the
damage they may cause.
Next, we discuss the considered threats and their countermeasures under a
hostile network:
%
\begin{description}
\item [\S\ref{sec.design.byz}]
    Byzantine Peers:
    how self-verification reduces malicious peers to denial of service.
\item [\S\ref{sec.design.partitions.farming}]
    Reputation Farming:
    how tick squeezing bounds \reps minted in isolation.
\item [\S\ref{sec.design.partitions.hardforks}]
    Hard Forks:
    how local settled actions become immune to reordering.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Byzantine Peers}
\label{sec.design.byz}

A \emph{Byzantine peer}~\cite{lamport.byz} may serve forged actions, suppress
branches, or answer differently to different peers.
%
Nevertheless, since peers are not trusted a priori, and every action is part of
the tamper-proof DAG, any erratic data is detected at the first inconsistent
action,
allowing the receiver to drop the connection and blacklist the remote peer.

Therefore, a Byzantine peer cannot impose forged content or prevent other peers
from synchronizing directly: it can only deny service, simulating a partition
that correct peers can still route around.

Unlike classic Byzantine agreement with permissioned
quorums~\cite{lamport.byz,p2p.byz},
in \FC nothing is decided by counting peers, only by validating each action
locally and ordering branches by the reputation of their members
(\S\ref{sec.design.consensus}).

\subsubsection{Reputation Farming}
\label{sec.design.partitions.farming}

Back in Figure~\ref{fig.reps}.A, consider a malicious scenario in which member
$c$ intentionally disconnects from the network for weeks to cultivate fake
identities $x$ and $y$ that accumulate \reps from junk posts every 24 hours
(\R{1.b}).
%
The intention is that when merging, these Sybils would become legitimate and
now hold the majority of \reps.

However, this attack is ineffective in the losing branch
(Figure~\ref{fig.reps}.B), since their posts are all squeezed into the same
tick, which can only produce \nreps{1000} per identity (\R{1.b}).
Therefore, not only farming fails, but member $c$ cannot afford most of the
posts, which are invalidated.

\subsubsection{Hard Forks}
\label{sec.design.partitions.hardforks}

Back in Figure~\ref{fig.reps}.A, as a counterpoint, suppose members $a$ and $b$
are the ones who have abandoned the forum for months.
This would invert the outcome and make \code{branch-1} with $c$, $x$ and $y$
legitimate.
Nevertheless, members $a$ and $b$ would succeed in taking over the forum, since
they control the majority of \reps in the common history, which corresponds to
the \emph{long-range attack} in proof-of-stake~\cite{pos.weak}.

Even if both branches are legitimate and were genuinely disconnected for a
long period, it is unacceptable that an active forum is overridden by a
long-absent branch.
%
From the local perspective, a remote branch that forged its history is
indistinguishable from one that returns from a long absence.
Therefore, reputation alone becomes a fragile criterion for consensus.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\columnwidth]{hard}
\caption{
    Settling window over consensus timeline.
}
\label{fig.hard}
\end{figure}

As a countermeasure, \FC protects settled local branches from outdated actions,
which corresponds to checkpoints in proof-of-stake~\cite{pos.weak}.
%
As Figure~\ref{fig.hard} illustrates, actions settle 7 days after they enter
the local order, counting in position timestamps in the consensus timeline
(\S\ref{sec.design.time}).
A branch whose fork point is unsettled is always merged (\code{ok}).
However, settled fork points only merge losing branches (\code{lose}), but
never winning branches (\code{win}).
%
The last case, which is analogous to a hard fork in Bitcoin, makes the syncing
peers incompatible, protecting \code{branch-1} from $a$ and $b$ in the scenario
above.
Since position timestamps cannot be forged or accelerated, peers holding the
same DAG reach the same verdict.
As minor caveats, idle forums never settle their most recent actions, and
winning branches keep their own dates, settling as soon as they merge.

In summary, the consensus rules to merge a local branch \code{L} with a remote
branch \code{R} are as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \code{L} wins:
        \code{L} is first, \code{R} is second
        (same as \S\ref{sec.design.consensus}).
    \item \code{R} wins and affects unsettled \code{L}:
        \code{R} is first, \code{L} is second
        (same as \S\ref{sec.design.consensus}).
    \item \code{R} wins and affects settled \code{L}:
        \code{L} is first, \code{R} is rejected
        (hard fork rule).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Convergence and Divergence}
\label{sec.design.sec}

The consensus mechanism aims at \emph{strong eventual consistency}, which
requires eventual delivery, strong convergence, and
termination~\cite{p2p.crdts}.
%
Eventual delivery holds within the settling window or when the local branch
wins (\S\ref{sec.design.partitions.hardforks}), a deliberate limit to protect
active forums from outdated branches.
%
Strong convergence holds by construction, since the replay algorithm
(\S\ref{sec.design.consensus}) uses pure evaluations to compute durations,
\reps, and consensus over the DAG, which only grows by appends and unions.
%
Termination also holds by construction, since the replay algorithm
visits each action only once.

To conclude, since consensus rests on subjective ratings, forks are legitimate
outcomes rather than failures.
Unlike Bitcoin, forums may split and diverge freely, and members may even fork
deliberately to ``reboot'' a community from a previous state.
%
The consensus algorithm does not prevent such splits, but makes them
transparent and deterministic, so that members can understand the evolution of
a forum and act accordingly.
Ultimately, a hard fork is a social conflict, not a numeric dispute.

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec.evaluation}

We evaluate the performance and robustness of \FC against the actual history of
real online forums.
We take public forum archives and simulate them as a new chain: every message
is replayed with its original time and author, but this time as a post under
the protocol rules, as if the forum had always run on it.
%
Unlike synthetic workloads, these archives carry real human activity: bursts
and silences, a few prolific authors among many occasional ones, and years of
accumulated content.
Over these simulations, we measure post and sync times, storage, forks and
convergence, and the economy of \reps.

We selected four public archives, each representing a scenario with its own
challenges:

\begin{description}
\item [\S\ref{sec.eval.usenet}]
    Usenet Newsgroup:
    how local-first peers converge after deliberate offline periods.
\item [\S\ref{sec.eval.chat}]
    Online Chat:
    how the protocol behaves under real-time activity.
\item [\S\ref{sec.eval.github}]
    GitHub Issues:
    how votes highlight content and distribute \reps.
\item [\S\ref{sec.eval.lemmy}]
    Federated Community:
    whether awarded \reps can afford real moderation actions.
\end{description}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{topo}
\caption{
    Network topology for the simulations.
}
\label{fig.topo}
\end{figure}

All simulations share the same network topology with 59 peers, as illustrated
in Figure~\ref{fig.topo}:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item
    70 primary links (gray) and 42 backup links (light dotted), with a diameter
    of 6 hops (e.g., \code{45-M9-S5-S4-S3-M7-43}).
\item
    5 \emph{super peers} in a ring (\code{S1}--\code{S5}), with \code{S1} fully
    connected.
\item
    9 \emph{middle peers} in a backup ring (\code{M1}--\code{M9}), each
    attached to 1--3 supers.
\item
    45 \emph{leaf peers} (\code{01}--\code{45}), split into groups of 1--15
    siblings, each attached to a primary middle (33 with backups).
\end{itemize}
%
This core-periphery shape is typical of peer-to-peer and federated
networks~\cite{p2p.survey,p2p.overlay,p2p.skype,p2p.monero,p2p.mastodon,p2p.flocking}.
%
Despite the group names, all peers run the same unmodified \FC software,
differing only in the number of connections and synchronization schedule:
%
\begin{itemize}
\item
    \emph{Green peers} are always online, except for their churn rate of 1\%
    (supers) and 5\% (others).
\item
    \emph{Yellow peers} connect every $\sim$6h (middles) and $\sim$24h
    (leaves), with a churn rate of 10\%.
    They synchronize, post, synchronize again, and disconnect.
\item
    \emph{Red peers} are local-first peers posting offline and connecting
    periodically like yellow peers.
\end{itemize}
%
In the context of \FC, links and connectivity are abstract: peers keep no open
connections, but only synchronize point-to-point during online periods
(connect, sync, disconnect).

Each simulation distributes authors uniformly over the leaves and replays all
original messages in a fresh chain as signed posts by their authors, issued
from their peers at the declared times.
A peer that receives new posts pushes them through its links after a short
random relay delay (1--60 seconds).
The topology dictates the simulation steps: peers synchronize only along their
links and according to their schedules.
To handle churn, offline periods, and network partitions, the simulation
driver (outside \FC) includes an internal queueing mechanism for pending posts.

As expected, all peers in all simulations converge to the same final state and
consensus order.
%
Hard forks (\S\ref{sec.design.partitions.hardforks}) never occur, since peers
reconnect far below the 7-day window, making late branches unlikely.
We do not force hard forks either, since they are an orthogonal (and
subjective) safeguard that would only split the simulation.

Next we describe the metrics we measure over the simulations:
%
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Storage:} on-disk size of the packed Git repository.
    \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Payloads:}   all combined (same as cleaned archive)
    \item \emph{Commits:}    all actions combined
    \item \emph{Snapshots:}  all per-action state combined
    \item \emph{Overhead:}   total size over Payloads
    \end{enumerate}
\item \emph{Operations:} cost of protocol operations.
    \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Post:}       local commit and validation time
    \item \emph{Sync:}       local pull and validation time
    \item \emph{Commits:}    commits per sync
    \item \emph{Bytes:}      bytes per sync
    \end{enumerate}
\item \emph{Propagation:} delay for a post to reach all peers.
\item \emph{Begs:} ratio of posts that require extra begs (after admission).
\item \emph{Forks:} ratio of posts that fork the chain.
    \begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Classes:}    ratio by peer class
    \item \emph{Missing:}    earlier posts a forking post lacks
    \item \emph{Reorders:}   actions reordered per merge
    \item \emph{Voided:}     actions voided per merge
    \item \emph{Clamps:}     clamped posts ($P_i > S_i$), and by how much
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

Each \code{sync} runs locally, between repositories in the same disk, to
avoid network noise.
Nevertheless, the relay delay (30s on average) already overestimates network
propagation times.
%
In terms of runtime, recall that a \code{sync} extends a \code{git push} with a
hook (\S\ref{sec.git}) that replays and verifies remote posts from the common
ancestor and computes the consensus order (\S\ref{sec.design.consensus}).

We run all simulations on a single standard desktop PC:
%
\begin{description}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec.related}

Permissionless consensus is notably
challenging~\cite{permissionless,blockchain.wild} to the point that
decentralized forums~\cite{p2p.ecosystem} settle for weaker forms:
%
\begin{description}
\item [\S\ref{sec.related.users}]
\emph{Multi-User/Single-Node} forums rely on trusted servers to order and
moderate messages consistently among members (e.g., federated
protocols~\cite{p2p.ecosystem}).
\item [\S\ref{sec.related.nodes}]
\emph{Single-User/Multi-Node} feeds support consistency across machines, but
restricted to a single writer (e.g., Scuttlebutt~\cite{p2p.scuttlebutt}).
\end{description}

We also discuss alternatives closer to our proposal:
%
\begin{description}
\item [\S\ref{sec.related.multi}]
\emph{Multi-User/Multi-Node} forums pursue consistency across peers with no
trusted authorities (e.g., social blockchains~\cite{blockchain.osn}).
\item [\S\ref{sec.related.reps}]
    Reputation systems~\cite{rep.systems} allow members to judge content and
    other members by their past behavior.
\end{description}

\subsection{Multi-User/Single-Node Forums}
\label{sec.related.users}

Federated protocols, such as e-mail and Usenet~\cite{fed.usenet}, allow users
of one domain to exchange messages with users of other domains asynchronously.

Modern equivalents include \emph{Mastodon}~\cite{p2p.mastodon,p2p.flocking} and
\emph{Lemmy}~\cite{forum.lemmy}, which rely on
ActivityPub~\cite{pubsub.activitypub} as a standard publish-subscribe model
with no consistency guarantees.
%
\emph{Matrix}~\cite{fed.matrix} targets real-time collaboration (e.g., chats)
and consistently replicates each room as a DAG of events across servers, but
with no consensus order.

As drawbacks, federations fall under the \emph{single-node} authority each user
trusts:
    identities are not portable across domains~\cite{fed.distributed}, and
    messages crossing boundaries may be subject to different
    policies~\cite{p2p.ecosystem}, up to whole servers blocking each
    other~\cite{fed.defederation,fed.blocklists}.
%
As a pragmatic mitigation, Matrix supports a permissioned moderation system%
\footnote{Matrix moderation:
    \url{https://matrix.org/docs/communities/moderation/}},
which, however, applies reactively after abusive messages flood the network.

In friend-to-friend networks, such as Retroshare~\cite{p2p.retroshare} and
Briar~\cite{p2p.briar}, nodes relay posts from friends to friends
transitively, with no order or shared verdict.
%
Therefore, they are similar to federations in the sense that each node applies
local policies and decides whom to connect to, i.e., each user is their own
server.

\subsection{Single-User/Multi-Node Forums}
\label{sec.related.nodes}

On the other side of the spectrum, \emph{single-user} feeds, such as
Scuttlebutt~\cite{p2p.scuttlebutt}, Nostr~\cite{p2p.nostr.nip01}, and
Bluesky~\cite{p2p.atproto}, move the authority from domains to users.
This weakens the essence of public forums as shared spaces, since each user has
complete sovereignty over an isolated unit of replication.

For instance, Scuttlebutt is designed around public identities that follow each
other to form a graph of connections, which shapes both the network topology
and what each peer stores:
    if identity $A$ follows identity $B$, it means that the computer of $A$
    connects to $B$'s and also that it stores all of their posts locally.
%
For shared spaces, Scuttlebutt supports channels (e.g., \emph{\#sports}), which
aggregate tagged posts into local virtual feeds.
In practice, channels simply merge friends' posts and filter them by tags,
hindering new users from integrating because their posts have no visibility
at all.
As a counterpoint, channels are safe places that rarely suffer from abuse.

Nostr imposes no shared structure, and each identity publishes its messages to
multiple servers, so that other users can fetch them.
All ordering, consistency, and moderation are therefore left to applications.
%
In contrast, Bluesky recovers a global view, halfway to federations, by
aggregating known feeds into larger indexes, which become the authority for
ordering and moderation.

\subsection{Multi-User/Multi-Node Forums}
\label{sec.related.multi}

In their most general form, forums aim to provide consistency across peers with
no trusted authorities, which makes Sybils the fundamental challenge to
address.
%
We identify two common approaches to combat Sybils:
either make the network permissioned, or rely on an external resource for
scarcity.

Collaborative local-first software~\cite{p2p.local,p2p.automerge,p2p.pushpin}
relies on conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs) as a
robust foundation for consistent concurrent updates~\cite{p2p.crdts}.
However, CRDTs alone cannot address content volume or quality and assume honest
(permissioned) replicas only~\cite{p2p.merkle-crdts,p2p.byz,p2p.dag.sync}.
%
Autobase~\cite{p2p.autobase} merges single-user Dat feeds~\cite{p2p.dat} into
multi-user/multi-node causal DAGs, which are eventually consistent but depend
on quorums of permissioned indexers.

Aether~\cite{p2p.ecosystem} relies on proof-of-work for every post, which
rate-limits floods without any authority, a dubious defense given the hardware
asymmetry in heterogeneous networks.
%
Furthermore, Aether has no intention to enforce consensus: posts are ephemeral,
and the elected moderators of a community only provide defaults that any user
can impeach locally.

Social blockchains~\cite{dosn.navigating,blockchain.osn}, such as
Steem/Hive~\cite{p2p.steem} and Farcaster~\cite{p2p.farcaster.imc}, deliver
consensus, but rely on cryptocurrencies for scarcity with inherent drawbacks
(\S\ref{sec.introduction}):
    admission and votes can be purchased~\cite{p2p.steemit.bots},
    power concentrates up to domination (e.g., the Steem takeover), and
    posts (payloads) are immutable by design.

\FC takes a third approach in which scarcity comes from the reputation earned
inside the forum, and consensus comes from the members in proportion to that
reputation.

\subsection{Reputation Systems}
\label{sec.related.reps}

In peer-to-peer file sharing, reputation ranks sources, but takes no part
in storing or ordering content.
%
A Bayesian trust model~\cite{p2p.rep.wang} keeps a local view, in which a peer
scores others based on past experiences and on recommendations of its trust
circle.
%
EigenTrust~\cite{rep.eigentrust} computes instead a global view that reflects
the experiences of all peers in the network, but depends on pre-trusted
(permissioned) peers to guarantee convergence and break up malicious
collectives.

In public forums, members earn reputation from votes on their posts.
%
Slashdot grants moderation points only to users with positive
karma~\cite{forum.slashdot}, and Reddit makes karma the main reward of
participation~\cite{forum.reddit.karma}, but both under a central server.
%
Social blockchains derive reputation from wealth rather than
contributions~\cite{p2p.steem.wealth}.

In our permissionless context, two previous results indicate how
reputation can resist Sybils:
%
since identities are free, members can easily discard bad reputations, so
newcomers must start with the lowest rank~\cite{rep.pseudonyms}; and
since Sybils can rate each other, only scores flowing from trusted
parties are Sybil-resistant~\cite{rep.sybilproof,sybil.guard,sybil.limit}.

\FC complies with both:
    newcomers start with no \reps, and \reps flow from pioneers to
    contributing members, always through taxed likes (penalizing Sybils that
    like each other).
%
Unlike all of the above, reputation in \FC derives from consensus over the
content itself.
In turn, reputation admits members and determines the order and moderation
of posts.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec.conclusion}

\iffalse
In this paper, we propose a permissionless consensus and reputation mechanism
for social content sharing in peer-to-peer networks.
We enumerate four main contributions:
    (i)   human authored content as a scarce resource
          (\emph{proof-of-authoring});
    (ii)  diversified public forums, each as an independent blockchain with
          subjective moderation rules;
    (iii) abusive content removal preserving data integrity; and
    (iv)  three-layered CRDTs to build collaborative applications.

The key insight of the consensus mechanism is to use the human authoring
ability as a scarce resource to determine consensus.
This contrasts with extrinsic resources, such as CPU power, which are
dispendious and not evenly distributed among people.
%
Consensus is backed by a reputation system in which members can rate posts with
likes and dislikes, which transfer reputation between them.
The only way to forge reputation is by authoring new content under the
judgement of other members.
This way, reputation generation is expensive, while verification is cheap and
decentralized.

The reputation and consensus mechanism is integrated into \FC, a peer-to-peer
protocol, in which members can create public forums of interest and apply diverse
moderation policies.
In particular, members can revoke content considered abusive according to the
majority, not depending on centralized authorities.
%
We simulate the behavior of existing chat and newsgroup forums as if they were
using \FC to show the practicability of the protocol as a descentralized
alternative for public forums.

On top of the proposed three-layered CRDT architecture, we prototyped a
decentralized version control system that resolves commit conflicts
automatically:
    (i)   at the transport layer, the full commit history is held in a
          Merkle~DAG counting as a CvRDT;
    (ii)  at the application layer, the commit patches operate as (mostly)
          commutative CmRDT operations; and
    (iii) after consensus is applied, merge conflicts are resolved
          automatically.

Finally, we do not claim that the proposed reputation system enforces ``good''
human behavior in any way.
Instead, it provides a transparent and quantitative mechanism to help members
understand the evolution of forums and act accordingly.
Human creativity contrasts with plain economic resources (e.g.,
proof-of-work), which do not appraise social interactions and also tend
to concentrate power over the time.
\fi
